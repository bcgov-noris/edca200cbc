The Canadian Broadcasting Corporation (CBC)

90 years of adult education over the airwaves

- CBC started as the CRBC (1932): economic pressures due to the Great Depression, worry about invading American culture as radio had no borders. - Public radio with the goal of national unity and preserving a Canadian identity, at arm's length from government. - Geographical distances could be bridged by radio. - Education was mentioned by Bennett in launching the CRBC. - In this comparison, we'll look at two radio shows: Citizens' Forum, which aired 1943 to 1964, and Ideas, which started in 1965 and continues today, more than 6 decades later. *(Changed "mid-1950s" to "1964" per Goodman, 2016, and English, 2026.)* - Quote: "The inauguration in 1936 of a national radio system, the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation (CBC) that could "span instantaneously the great Canadian spaces" provided the ideal means by which the urgent issues could be shared and mobilized via mass communication. Proponents of adult education and rural reform were well aware that social change would only be brought about if sufficient numbers of people could be brought together to discuss issues they considered important." (Sandwell, 2012, p. 6)

The CBC then: Citizens' Forum

- Citizens Forum was modeled after the National Farm Radio Forum that was also airing on CBC at the time. It used the same idea of listening groups and pamphlets distributed before a program to encourage discussion among the listening groups. It was meant to be a more urban focus with less of a specific audience, and it was particularly interested in planning for the post-war era. - Running from 1943 to 1964, the Citizens' Forum show was run jointly by the CAAE and the CBC. The CAAE wrote the study materials, while CBC chose the speakers and topics and aired the show. (Goodman, 2016; Romanow, 2005)

- The context was the Great Depression and the time between the two world wars. There was a great concern for national identity in Canada and a sense of threat from the our American neighbor who was sending their cultural identity across the border using radio and other means. The decision to use radio and form a public broadcaster was an effort to preserve Canadian identity and stimulate national discussions using a medium that could travel from coast to coast to coast. (Romanow, 2005; Vipond, 2013) - To add: one sentence on WWII and postwar planning (Citizens' Forum started in 1943). - Feature quote: "The specific plans for the Citizens Forum (CF) grew from a conference of adult educators and the CBC in September 1943, discussing how to involve citizens in planning for postwar reconstruction." (Goodman, 2016, p. 631)

- Quote 1: "People, we are told, are curious animals. The problem for governors is how to satisfy that curiosity in ways that benefit the individual and the society. Beginning roughly in the years just after the close of the Great War, idealists and educators worked through a collection of agencies to realize their dreams of an enlightened populace." (Rutherford, 1990b, p. 153) *(Added a missing space after "told,". Page converted from PDF p. 5, since the chapter runs pp. 149–187; check against the book page.)* - Quote 2: "There was a terrible earnestness about the rhetoric of adult education. Knowledge wasn't just its own reward; it was a source of power and progress as well. Educators and their allies feared that the public lacked sufficient information to manage their own lives properly as well as to run an increasingly complex democracy." (Rutherford, 1990b, p. 153) - To write: my bullets, naming Week 2 Model 1 (individual ↔ society). - (Q3 my words) The purpose of the Citizens Forum strongly fits Model 1 as described in our course materials, looking at a continuum from individual to society. The Citizens Forum explicitly looked at developing individual intellectual skills in order to benefit society, to increase national capacity and community. (CACE Program, University of Victoria, n.d.-a; English, 2026, p. 36)

- Organized listening groups prepared for each episode of the radio show. They would get study bulletins that were prepared and mailed out to them. They would listen to the radio show, discuss the topic in their group, and then send their reports back to a provincial office. Each show included a panel discussion of some kind. Later shows also included audience questions, and each show also included time for provincial reports. These were live broadcasts from different cities, and the forum really discouraged the idea of there being two sides to any argument, preferring to develop all aspects of an issue. Occasionally, there was pushback for the topics they covered, and especially around fears of promoting communism in the later shows. (Goodman, 2016, p. 632; Romanow, 2005, p. 117; English, 2026, p. 54) - Feature quote: "The audience was encouraged to listen and discuss the program in their own location. It was a simple concept that involved complex work." (English, 2026, p. 35)

- Citizens' Forum feels progressive, similar to Farm Radio Forum, but with less of a practical focus as it didn't have the action elements of FRF. They did have feedback. They didn't have a clear audience, and the CBC restricted the topics to avoid controversy. Though the government/business still felt they were too left-leaning in the panel discussions. Some elements of transformation (critical reflection). They talk about changing attitudes and knowledge can't be unlearned, so those bring in a bit of a liberal/humanist flavour. [tidy later] - Supporting: the 1943 Manifesto made the CAAE "a more progressive and active organization" (English, 2026, p. 38); controversy reason (Romanow, 2005, p. 117); knowledge can't be unlearned (Romanow, 2005, p. 117).

- Though it ran for 20 years, the last half of its run was marked by a sharp decline of listening groups. Even by 1950, there were only 400 groups left, down from the 1,200 that it had in its first year. They attempted to do the Citizens Forum as a TV show, but it didn't translate very well and only lasted one season. The arrival of TV broadcasts more generally and the lack of a specific audience contributed to the show's decline. People started feeling more awkward listening to radio shows in the community groups and started to stay at home more, listening to the radio and watching TV in their own family groups. (Romanow, 2005, p. 118; Rutherford, 1990b; Goodman, 2016, p. 634; English, 2026, pp. 59, 61) - The enthusiasm for postwar planning that was so key in 1943 wasn't sustainable by the 50s and 60s. The world had changed, and there were more fears of political controversy, including concerns that the show was promoting communism. The shift in public appetite was more for individual and family-based discussion and reflection versus larger groups. (English, 2026, pp. 54, 59; Goodman, 2016, p. 634) - Feature quote: "it did not die of attack – it died of attrition." (Isabel Wilson, as cited in English, 2026, p. 59)

- It's interesting that Citizens' Forum feels sort of like a secondary forum to the Farm Radio Forum, because that one seemed more successful. - My guess is that FRF was more successful because of the audience targeting and the community building element. It shows how education and community building really go together. - Citizens' Forum still managed to bring a lot of interesting questions into discussion at the national level. - They did the program in front of a live audience and took Q&As from the audience, and included elements from community discussions from previous weeks. - Citizens' Forum showed that Canadians were interested in having conversations, even though our geography and our diversity mean that we don't always speak together easily. - Feature quote options: "Listening groups played a role in propagating the historically-novel idea that all citizens should have opinions – evidence-based, defendable in robust discussion about national and world affairs." (Goodman, 2016, p. 644) / "looking through the more than three hundred bulletins produced, one can see that Canadian national concerns have changed little." (English, 2026, p. 49)

The CBC now: Ideas

- Ideas started in 1965, and it was a classic example of the CBC trying to save some money. In order to do that, they merged two existing programs, one that was called University of the Air, which was a series of lectures, and another called The Learning Stage. Those two programs were run by Phyllis Webb and Bill Young, and they were the first producers or creators of Ideas. It's still running today, hosted by Nahlah Ayed, and runs Monday to Friday on CBC Radio One. It's about an hour-long show, and it's also released as a podcast. It has a variety of formats: documentaries, lectures, interviews, panels, and it also has a key role every year by broadcasting the Massey Lectures. (Lucht, 2005, p. 7; Massey College, 2021) [cite CBC's Ideas page for today's host and schedule] - To add: it was "born as a program of adult education" (Massey College, 2021, 05:51). - Feature quote options: "[Webb] and Young worked together on a proposal to merge University of the Air and The Learning Stage to create a new nightly program that would take the best of each series and present adult education on radio using a variety of approaches." (Lucht, 2005, p. 7) / ""Ideas . . . The Best Ideas You'll Hear Tonight," intoned CBC announcer Ken Haslam. With those words, spoken in his cultivated baritone voice, Haslam launched the first episode of what was described as "the new look in CBC educational broadcasting," an hour-long show of "radio for the mind," to be heard each weeknight. It was Monday, October 24, 1965." (Lucht, 2005, p. 6) *(Date conflict: October 24, 1965 was a Sunday. Lucht says October 25 in the Massey Dialogues video, which was a Monday. If using the date, say October 25 or just "October 1965.")*

- From that first description on the very first show as being radio for the mind, Ideas has continued to have a thought-provoking, engaging and informational purpose. It is a way of provoking public discussion, though it doesn't include listening groups like its predecessor. It does often get recorded with a live audience and encourages people to talk together about these ideas. Lucht, a longtime producer of the show, says that the show should induce doubt in listeners' minds. So he thinks it works best when it challenges an assumption or makes you pause and rethink. (Lucht, 2005, pp. 6, 11; Massey College, 2021) - To check: "often recorded with a live audience" in the first paragraph (only the Massey Lectures are confirmed live). Soften to "sometimes." - Similar to the Citizens Forum, Ideas also fits into the Model 1 continuum, looking at individual through society, and has more of an individual focus because of its delivery through radio and podcasts to smaller audiences. But it also has the same goal of engaging people to think and become part of the wider societal dialogue. The Massey Lectures are delivered to a live audience at various venues and then shared on the Ideas radio broadcasts. The 2026 edition of 5 lectures actually starts the same day this assignment is due. (CACE Program, University of Victoria, n.d.-a) [cite a CBC Massey Lectures page for the 2026 dates] - Feature quote: Ideas, he said, was about helping to "locate ourselves in the universe." (Lucht, 2005, p. 8)

These days there are fewer long projects, of course, budget constraints, but also attention spans. - At its core, these two radio shows represent a common experience for Canadians, listening to topics that showcase the breadth and depth of our population from coast to coast to coast, as they like to say. Now, people call in to talk back or leave comments on podcast episodes to have their say. There are still people gathering to generally watch CBC in big groups, for example, in the Olympics: watching the Olympics on the big screen in a library, or watching a sports game or an episode of Schitt's Creek in a family group in their houses. We still listen and watch together. Why it matters for adult educators

- I'm somebody who likes to process verbally, so these kinds of forums and discussion groups would have worked well for me. - Sometimes they say talk is cheap, but this oral tradition and discussion in a group, especially if you can find people who are not thinking exactly the same as you, is still so valuable. - You can see it even in this course: a large element of it is discussion based. It's about asking good questions. - One of the early pieces talks about the importance of framing really excellent questions for the discussion groups, especially if the leaders are not experienced facilitators. It really speaks to the value of asking good questions. ("a series of provocative discussion questions" (Sandwell, 2012, p. 9)) - There's also a piece about being open to being challenged, and making sure that discussion doesn't prevent action, but is a precursor or prerequisite to action. - Active versus passive reception: have we shifted to being really passive in our consumption of podcasts or radio? I think the CBC and others still do a really good job of inviting listeners to interact and participate together. - The struggle: the very muddy parts where it feels like it's never going to come together, and then it does. There's something about that struggle that really has an impact on your creativity and your learning. It's uncomfortable, especially in the middle of the process, but it is vital. - I think the struggle brings it into ourselves. It's time spent, turning it around in our minds, sleeping on it, letting it simmer and connect to other threads. An instant answer doesn't have such an impact. - Course links to name: constructivism (new learning builds on existing understanding; CACE Program, University of Victoria, n.d.-c); "active participants and not mere passive recipients" (Goodman, 2016, p. 629). - Why it matters: my adult educator takeaway (November 15) - I think one of my biggest takeaways from the adult education aspects, especially of the Citizens Forum and its earlier counterpart, is that audio can complement other aspects of learning in this way. Just because you're delivering something sort of lecture style from one broadcast point out to communities doesn't mean that the reception has to be passive. Incorporating other forms of learning, like distributing materials in advance, having discussion groups, bringing in social aspects, building a whole package around what could have just been a lecture over the radio, really deepens the engagement with the subject matter. - I'm somebody who's a verbal processor, so as I said, this format would probably have worked quite well for me: small group discussion, social time. - In the modern era, we've had to find new ways to do it, but you can see that people are still interested in these discussions, still show up to be in the audience of the Massey Lectures, still put on a podcast while they clean the bathroom. People want to talk, want to think, and we just have to meet them with flexibility and support them to work through ideas on their own and in their communities. - Supporting: "adult educationists make it their business to help the citizens to become active participants and not mere passive recipients" (Goodman, 2016, p. 629)
