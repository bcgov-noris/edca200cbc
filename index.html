The Canadian Broadcasting Corporation (CBC)

90 years of adult education over the airwaves

In 1932, Prime Minister R. B. Bennett created the Canadian Radio Broadcasting Commission (CRBC) and Parliament passed the Radio Broadcasting Bill (Romanow, 2005, p. 111). Responding to economic pressures created by the Great Depression and cultural threats from the United States, Bennett called radio "a most effective instrument to nation building, with an education value difficult to estimate" (Faris, 1975, p. 70, as cited in Romanow, 2005, p. 111).

Four years later, the CRBC became the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation (CBC), Canada's national radio system (Romanow, 2005, p. 113).

Proponents of adult education and rural reform were well aware that social change would only be brought about if sufficient numbers of people could be brought together to discuss issues they considered important.

(Sandwell, 2012, p. 6)

In this presentation, we'll look at two CBC radio shows through the lens of adult education: Citizens' Forum, which aired from 1943 to 1964, and Ideas, which launched in 1965 and is still going strong, more than six decades later.

The CBC then: Citizens' Forum

Listen: "Is Canada a Nation Divided Against Itself?"

Citizens' Forum · 24:51 (CBC/Radio-Canada, 1944)

This early episode of the Citizens' Forum is a scripted discussion about nationhood and the constitution with a panel of lawyers and moderator Morley Callaghan.

Listen to the Citizens' Forum on the CBC website

From the CAAE perspective, the Citizens’ Forum was to be more open-ended than the Farm Forum. The Farm Forum was more about making farming better – an action research project. In contrast, the Citizens’ Forum was intended to reach an urban audience and generate enlightened conversation, especially about the future.

(English, 2026, p. 58)

Citizens' Forum was modeled after the National Farm Radio Forum (NFRF) that was also airing on CBC at the time. The model included distributing educational materials to listening groups prior to each program for discussion, as well as collecting group feedback to share as part of a future show (Romanow, 2005, p. 117; Goodman, 2016, p. 632).

The show had more of an urban audience than the NFRF and was started with the goal of planning for a post-war era (English, 2026, p. 58; Goodman, 2016, p. 631).

Running from 1943 to 1964, the Citizens' Forum show was run jointly by the Canadian Association for Adult Education (CAAE) and the CBC. The CAAE wrote the study materials, while CBC chose the speakers and topics and produced the show (Goodman, 2016, pp. 631–632, 635).

The specific plans for the Citizens Forum (CF) grew from a conference of adult educators and the CBC in September 1943, discussing how to involve citizens in planning for postwar reconstruction.

(Goodman, 2016, p. 631)

While the CBC launched during the Great Depression and in response to a concern for national unity, the Citizens' Forum was started to prepare Canadians for the ending of World War II (Romanow, 2005, p. 115).

The shifting national context was in part because "[w]ars are very good for national unity" (Romanow, 2005, p. 113). While it was a difficult time to be looking forward, the CAAE hoped that a shared understanding of the problems facing democracy would lead to a better society and move away from the threat of fascism (Romanow, 2005, p. 115).

People, we are told, are curious animals. The problem for governors is how to satisfy that curiosity in ways that benefit the individual and the society. Beginning roughly in the years just after the close of the Great War, idealists and educators worked through a collection of agencies to realize their dreams of an enlightened populace.

(Rutherford, 1990, p. 153)

The purpose of the Citizens' Forum strongly fits Model 1 as described in our course materials, as it was addressing the continuum from individual to society (CACE Program, University of Victoria, n.d.-a). The Citizens' Forum explicitly looked at developing individual intellectual skills in order to benefit society, to increase national capacity and strengthen community (Rutherford, 1990, p. 153).

Rutherford described educators' concern that the public didn't have the knowledge they would need to manage their own lives in a complex world: "There was a terrible earnestness about the rhetoric of adult education. Knowledge wasn't just its own reward; it was a source of power and progress as well" (1990, p. 153).

[D]iscussion, like democracy itself, is based on the idea that no one has the key to truth, but that a tolerable working compromise may emerge when a group of people, adequately informed, have plenty of opportunity to talk things over.

(UNESCO report, as cited in
Sandwell, 2012, p. 185)

The concept was simple, but the work to prepare for each episode was complex (English, 2026, p. 35). The CAAE prepared study materials and mailed them to organized listening groups in advance of each episode of the radio show. The groups would listen to the radio show together, discuss the topic in their group, and then send their reports back to a provincial office (Goodman, 2016, p. 632; Romanow, 2005, p. 117).

Each show included a panel discussion of some kind and was broadcast live from various cities. Later shows also included audience questions, and each show had dedicated time for provincial reports (Goodman, 2016, pp. 631-632; Romanow, 2005, p. 117).

The forum discouraged the idea of there being only two sides to any argument, preferring to develop all aspects of an issue. Occasionally, there was pushback for the topics they covered and their inclusion of speakers from multiple viewpoints, especially around fears of promoting communism in the later shows (Romanow, 2005, pp. 117–118; English, 2026, p. 54).

Action began with discussion. In fact it was often stated by the leaders that discussion is action; that ideas need not be restricted to the ivory tower, they can be put to work.

(Sim, as cited in Sandwell, 2012, p. 176)

The Citizens' Forum shared a progressive philosophy (English, 2026, p. 38) with the action-focused NFRF on which it was modelled, but its broader audience and national lens reduced its direct community impact (Romanow, 2005, p. 116).

By embracing the idea that discussion is the start of action, the program included some elements of a transformation philosophy through critical reflection (CACE Program, University of Victoria, n.d.-c).

Romanow's point that "knowledge cannot be unlearned" and that knowledge in and of itself represents change (2005, p. 117) brings in the self-actualization and growth focus of the humanist philosophy (CACE Program, University of Victoria, n.d.-b).

In her history of the forum, Isabel Wilson said that it did not die of attack – it died of attrition. [...] No matter what the forum tried in the 1950s, it could not sustain the vigour of the 1940s as social movements melted away and the war became a thing of the past.

(English, 2026, p. 59)

The enthusiasm for postwar planning that was so key in 1943 wasn't sustainable by the 1950s and 1960s. The world had changed, and there were more fears of political controversy, including concerns that the show was promoting communism. The shift in public appetite was more for individual and family-based discussion and reflection versus larger groups (English, 2026, pp. 54, 59; Goodman, 2016, p. 634).

Though the Citizens' Forum ran for 20 years, the last half of its run was marked by a sharp decline of listening groups. By 1950, there were only 400 groups left, down from the 1,200 that were formed in the show's first year. An attempt to transition the Citizens' Forum to TV didn't translate very well and only lasted one season (Rutherford, 1990, p. 174). The arrival of TV broadcasts more generally and the lack of a specific audience contributed to the show's decline. (Romanow, 2005, p. 118; Goodman, 2016, p. 634; English, 2026, pp. 59, 61)

Listening groups played a role in propagating the historically-novel idea that all citizens should have opinions – evidence-based, defendable in robust discussion about national and world affairs.

(Goodman, 2016, p. 644)

Althought the Citizens' Forum is less well-known than the National Farm Radio Forum, it still managed to bring a lot of interesting questions into discussion at the national level.

The NFRF had more support from voluntary organizations like the Canadian Federation of Agriculture (CFA), a more clear purpose and audience, and was better at building community through social groups and shared action (English, 2026, p. 59; Goodman, 2016, p. 634; Sandwell, 2012, p. 179).

Over its run, the Citizens' Forum produced more than three hundred bulletins, and English commented that "Canadian national concerns have changed little" (2026, p. 49).

Citizens' Forum showed that Canadians were interested in having conversations, even though our geography and our diversity mean that we don't always speak together easily.

The CBC now: Ideas

Featured episode: "Dispatches From Canada's Housing Crisis"

Ideas · 53 minutes (Ayed, 2026)

This hour-long episode features a conversation with the 2026 CBC Massey Lecturer, Leilani Farha. The Massey Lectures will air on Ideas with Nahlah Ayed, starting November 16, 2026.

Listen to Ideas on the CBC website

"Ideas . . . The Best Ideas You'll Hear Tonight," intoned CBC announcer Ken Haslam. With those words, spoken in his cultivated baritone voice, Haslam launched the first episode of what was described as "the new look in CBC educational broadcasting," an hour-long show of "radio for the mind," to be heard each weeknight.

(Lucht, 2005, p. 6)

Ideas started in 1965, and it was a classic example of the CBC trying to save some money (Massey College, 2021, 07:03). Show creators Phyllis Webb and Bill Young took the best of their two existing programs, University of the Air and The Learning Stage, merging them to create a new series to present "adult education on radio using a variety of approaches" (Lucht, 2005, p. 7).

Hosted by Nahlah Ayed, Ideas still runs as an hour-long show on Monday to Friday on CBC Radio One (CBC Radio, n.d.). Also released as a podcast, the show uses a variety of formats: documentaries, lectures, interviews, panels (Massey College, 2021, 13:44–14:46). It hosts the annual Massey Lectures series, which are recorded in front of a live audience (CBC/Radio-Canada, 2026a, p. 20).

Lister Sinclair, genius and polymath, an icon of Canadian culture and broadcasting, was the much-loved host of Ideas between 1983 and 1999. He described the purpose of the show elegantly and concisely: Ideas, he said, was about helping to “locate ourselves in the universe.”

(Lucht, 2005, p. 8)

From its very first show, Ideas has been described as being "radio for the mind" (Lucht, 2005, p. 6). It continues to have a thought-provoking, engaging and informational purpose. Like the Citizens' Forum before it, the show provokes public discussion (Massey College, 2021, 23:55–24:04), even without formal listening groups.

Ideas fits into the Model 1 continuum, looking at individual through society (CACE Program, University of Victoria, n.d.-a), and has more of an individual focus because of its delivery through radio and podcasts to smaller audiences. But it also has the same goal of engaging people to think and become part of the wider societal dialogue.

Lucht, a longtime producer of the show, says that the show should induce doubt in listeners' minds (2005, p. 11). So he thinks it works best when it challenges an assumption or makes you pause and rethink.

The best Ideas programs strike a chord. They help us recognize truths that may be felt but not yet articulated, the way good fiction or poetry does.

(Lucht, 2005, p. 14)

The philosophy driving Ideas is hard to pin down because the show is deliberately wide ranging. Thousands of speakers have appeared on Ideas over the years, exploring "an array of subjects that span the range of human thought, imagination, knowledge and experience" (Lucht, 2005, p. 8).

I think in the earlier years, Ideas was philosophically liberal, emphasizing the great ideas of the day. Over time, it has evolved, with recent producers seeing themselves as "facilitators of conversation" (Massey College, 2021, 40:02) rather than educators, putting the show into a humanist camp, with teacher as facilitator (CACE Program, University of Victoria, n.d.-b).

Ideas is the home of the annual Massey Lectures series. Named in honour [of] Vincent Massey, the first Canadian Governor General, the Massey Lectures began in 1961, conceived as a venue where prominent thinkers could explore significant contemporary ideas for Canadian audiences.

(Lucht, 2005, p. 9)

The Ideas producers have described how their ideas come from a large pool of contributors who are involved with the show. They have ideas pitched by freelance journalists and listeners, and each show is often strongly driven by an individual producer (Lucht, 2005, p. 11; Massey College, 2021, 44:31–47:46).

Earlier in its history, Ideas used to produce long series, for example, 20 or more episodes on one theme (Massey College, 2021, 13:44–14:46). These days there are fewer long projects due to budget constraints, the audience appetite for more concise content, plus competition in the learning space (Massey College, 2021, 37:52–39:04).

Today, Ideas is broadcast on live radio, on CBC Listen and as a podcast, and it continues to host the Massey Lectures (CBC Radio, n.d.; CBC/Radio-Canada, 2020).

Why it matters for adult educators

Making Ideas programs is an exercise in excitement, struggle, frustration, discovery and joy: the excitement of having an idea that compels you to want to share it with others; the struggle of working through the idea, clarifying it, finding out what it is about and how to best express it; the frustration that you can't seem to get it right; the discovery of new territory as you work on the idea and your understanding of it deepens; and the joy of breakthrough, a Zen-like moment when things become clear, the piece comes together, and you look back and wonder what all the fuss was about.

(Lucht, 2005, pp. 11–12)
- I'm somebody who likes to process verbally, so these kinds of forums and discussion groups would have worked well for me. - Sometimes they say talk is cheap, but this oral tradition and discussion in a group, especially if you can find people who are not thinking exactly the same as you, is still so valuable. - You can see it even in this course: a large element of it is discussion based. It's about asking good questions. - One of the early pieces talks about the importance of framing really excellent questions for the discussion groups, especially if the leaders are not experienced facilitators. It really speaks to the value of asking good questions. ("a series of provocative discussion questions" (Sandwell, 2012, p. 9)) - There's also a piece about being open to being challenged, and making sure that discussion doesn't prevent action, but is a precursor or prerequisite to action. - Active versus passive reception: have we shifted to being really passive in our consumption of podcasts or radio? I think the CBC and others still do a really good job of inviting listeners to interact and participate together. - The struggle: the very muddy parts where it feels like it's never going to come together, and then it does. There's something about that struggle that really has an impact on your creativity and your learning. It's uncomfortable, especially in the middle of the process, but it is vital. - I think the struggle brings it into ourselves. It's time spent, turning it around in our minds, sleeping on it, letting it simmer and connect to other threads. An instant answer doesn't have such an impact. - Course links to name: constructivism (new learning builds on existing understanding; CACE Program, University of Victoria, n.d.-c); "active participants and not mere passive recipients" (Goodman, 2016, p. 629). - Why it matters: my adult educator takeaway (November 15) - I think one of my biggest takeaways from the adult education aspects, especially of the Citizens Forum and its earlier counterpart, is that audio can complement other aspects of learning in this way. Just because you're delivering something sort of lecture style from one broadcast point out to communities doesn't mean that the reception has to be passive. Incorporating other forms of learning, like distributing materials in advance, having discussion groups, bringing in social aspects, building a whole package around what could have just been a lecture over the radio, really deepens the engagement with the subject matter. - I'm somebody who's a verbal processor, so as I said, this format would probably have worked quite well for me: small group discussion, social time. - In the modern era, we've had to find new ways to do it, but you can see that people are still interested in these discussions, still show up to be in the audience of the Massey Lectures, still put on a podcast while they clean the bathroom. People want to talk, want to think, and we just have to meet them with flexibility and support them to work through ideas on their own and in their communities. - Supporting: "adult educationists make it their business to help the citizens to become active participants and not mere passive recipients" (Goodman, 2016, p. 629)

Discussion question

The creation of the Citizens' Forum came during a time of "earnestness" about the power of adult education (Rutherford, 1990, p. 153). By contrast, in 2021, Ideas producer Naheed Mustafa commented that she prefers to see producers as "facilitators of conversation," calling the idea of education "paternalistic" (Massey College, 2021, 39:39–40:02).

Where do you see yourself on this spectrum? Do you identify as an educator or more as a facilitator?
